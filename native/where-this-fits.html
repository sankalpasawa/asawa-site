Where this fits, and how the future looks

The whole industry has quietly agreed on the same diagnosis: an AI without a governed picture of your business will confidently make things up about your business. That diagnosis is not our insight — it is now the sales pitch of enterprise software’s biggest categories. Where everyone differs is the treatment.

The categories, and what each one actually does

CategoryWhat it does about the problemWhat it does not do
Systems of record (ERP, CRM)Hold the authoritative dataDo not know how work is done — the process lives outside them
Process miningWatch your systems and reconstruct how work actually flowsObserve and score the process; do not run it, and do not check themselves
Ontology / AI operating layersBuild a mirror of the business so the AI stops hallucinating about itThe mirror describes work; the work still happens elsewhere
Workflow automationExecute predefined flowsNo model of the company around the flow — no notion of who owns what, or whether done was really done
Copilots / chat assistantsProduce output on demandRemember nothing structural; every session re-explains the company
Native’s position, in one line: the picture of the business should not just describe the work — it should BE the thing that runs the work, and it should check itself.

The difference is easiest to see on one concrete job. Take a monthly payment reconciliation:

A mirror of the businessNative
What existsA record that reconciliation happens, who does it, which systems it touchesThe reconciliation itself, written as steps with a declared “done means this” check
When it runsSomewhere else — a person, a script, a vendor toolThrough the written form; every run recorded
When something is offThe mirror finds out later, if the data flows backThe check fails, work stops, the owner is asked — with the evidence attached
Who audits itA person, occasionallyThe system re-tests its own playbooks on a schedule; a playbook that stops passing loses its standing

Self-checking as a screen, not a promise: the app’s own Evals tab — nightly score with honest exclusions, watchdog probes, and a rule that a check which already passes cannot claim new work.

Why now

  • Output became cheap. Models write, code and draft for everyone equally — output is rented. What a company can still own is its judgment: what to ask for, what good looks like, what to refuse. Judgment compounds only if it is written down. (Thesis — ours.)
  • Agents keep failing the same way. The field’s own diagnosis of agent failure is statelessness — nothing durable between sessions. And measured “context rot” kills the easy escape of ever-bigger windows. Production infrastructure is converging on the answer Native started from: keep the state in a record, not in the model. (External research; sourced below.)
  • Verification is where progress concentrates. AI improves fastest where checking is easy. Native’s whole design — every piece of work carries its own check — is that observation turned into architecture. (External observation; our application of it.)

The honest counter

One research bet could narrow our moat: memory architectures that move durable state inside the model. If that wins at scale, part of “the record is the state” loses force. Our counter-counter, stated plainly: even a model that remembers perfectly still isn’t accountable. A company needs permissions, audit trails, approval rights, and a source of truth it can inspect and take to a regulator — and those live outside any model, in exactly the kind of explicit record Native keeps. The moat narrows; the need does not vanish.

How the future looks from here

We tell the future as five chapters with proofs, not dates — a company’s recurring work running written and checked; then measurably improving; then departments trading work without human brokers; then whole efforts running reconstructably; then the system installing anywhere and growing on a stranger’s own record. The full story, with what’s proven so far and what each next step requires, is the Roadmap. How that spreads between companies — playbooks travel, your business never does — is Distribution.

If the thesis holds, the interesting consequence is not bigger companies doing more. It is small companies being properly run — the structure that used to require a floor of managers becoming something one person supervises. We run that experiment on ourselves daily: one operator, six companies’ operations. A load test, not a customer count — but it is the future this page bets on, at the smallest scale that can be tested.

Markets: interdisciplinary work

One core idea for the next five to six years: interdisciplinary work becomes one of the most powerful sectors. Until now, putting several disciplines on one problem meant putting several people in one room. A lending product, for example, needs a credit analyst, a data scientist, a compliance lawyer and a behavioural economist. Groups like that have always moved slowly: each side learns the others’ vocabulary, every handoff waits for a meeting, and the combined idea arrives late or not at all. Work between fields has been rare. It was never low-value; it simply cost too much to coordinate.

LLMs change that cost. One model can work in many fields at once, so one person or a small team can carry several disciplines in a single line of work. They can translate between fields and test an idea from one field against the rules of another, in hours rather than across months of meetings. The slow part of interdisciplinary work was coordinating people, and that part is getting cheap. (Thesis: founder’s, 2026-09-26. Short-to-medium horizon; not yet measured.)

For Native, this is the same bet seen from the other side. When each discipline is a department, work between fields becomes a handoff between departments, and the third chapter above (departments trading work without human brokers) is where that gets tested. (Our inference.)

Industry signals (sourced)

The named versions of the categories above, from our 2026 landscape research (38 sourced findings, status: research input, not doctrine): Palantir markets Ontology/AIP as the governed business model for AI; SAP grounds its assistant in a Knowledge Graph spanning ~452k tables; Celonis conformance-checks processes against its PI Graph; Gartner’s “digital twin of an organization” category mirrors but does not execute. In the scanned comparison, none both executes work as the model’s own units and re-verifies itself — that combination is the position Native claims.

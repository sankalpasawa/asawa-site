Futures at H1 HUMAN AND AGENT

Five chapters: the industry, the economy, needs by industry, the share agent orgs take, the next wants. Three futures with the event that picks each.

Futures at H1: five chapters

1
The industry. Four layers form: models, harnesses, AOs, and markets between AOs. Three or four model vendors, a handful of harnesses, millions of AOs. Sutra lives in the harness layer and sells the human seat.
2
The economy. Thinking work gets cheap and gets demanded more. What stays expensive is what needs a body, a signature, or trust. Payroll turns into a token bill plus a seat.
3
Human needs by industry. Every need keeps its industry; inside each, the paperwork, the search, the drafting and the coordination go to AOs, the touch and the liability stay with people.
4
The share. AOs take most of the checkable, bodiless, low-liability work by H1. Human orgs remain as seat holders, liability carriers and relationship owners: smaller, flatter, more owners and fewer managers.
5
The next wants. Each solved want makes a new one: provenance, curation, trust between AOs, fair trade, ownership of one's context, and the human-made premium. Those are where humans and AOs work together next.

Method: scenario planning (three futures with triggers, not one forecast); Max-Neef's nine needs; Baumol (what cannot be automated gets relatively dearer); Jevons (when a thing gets cheaper, more of it is used).

3.1 The industry: four layers form

Why this picture: four layers, because four different bills
  • Claim. The industry settles into four layers with very different counts.
  • Why four. Each layer has its own buyer and its own bill: models sell tokens, harnesses sell the department and the seat, AOs sell outcomes, markets sell trust between AOs. A layer that does not have its own bill folds into the one above.
  • Why the counts. Models consolidate on capital (training cost), so a few. Harnesses consolidate on trust and distribution, so a handful. AOs are one per owner by definition, so millions. Markets form only after many AOs exist, so late.
  • Evidence. Gartner's 40% of enterprise apps with agents by end 2026 and the agent market near 50 bn USD by 2030 are the layer below AOs growing. No analyst counts AOs yet.
  • What would change it. The third future: a model vendor ships org-grade departments and billing in-product. Then the harness layer collapses into the model layer and the picture has three bands.

Why three futures, and why these triggers
  • Why not one forecast. Scenario planning (Schwartz) says: name the few worlds that differ in what you should do, and the one public event that tells you which you are in. One forecast hides the decision; three with triggers make it.
  • Why these three. They differ on the one thing that matters to Sutra: who owns the seat. The harness (future 1), the consumer platform (future 2), or the model vendor (future 3).
  • Why these triggers. Each is observable by anyone: a public number beaten, a phone OS release, a model vendor's product launch used by a known company. A trigger you cannot see is not a trigger.
  • Evidence. April 2026's two business models (employees for hire; a CoS per person) are futures 1 and 2 under older names.

Projection Sutra is safe in the first two futures and squeezed in the third unless the thing it sells is not the harness but the seat: the one place the human stamps, pays and is liable.

3.2 The economy: thinking gets cheap, the body and trust get dearer

Why this picture: two old laws, one new line
  • Claim. The cost structure of a business inverts: the big block (payroll) becomes small, a new utility bill (tokens) appears, and the part that resists automation is what is left to pay for.
  • Baumol. When some work gets cheap, the work that cannot be automated gets relatively dearer. That is the dashed box: bodies and trust.
  • Jevons. When thinking gets cheaper, more of it is bought. That is why the H1 stack is taller, not shorter: more output, cheaper each.
  • Why the seat is a thin box. Its hours are few (one stamp per line in the runs) but its price per hour is high: it carries liability.
  • Evidence. McKinsey: 29.5% of US work hours automatable by 2030 with generative AI. Run 5: a web search at about 2 USD, a site in 4 minutes.
  • What would change it. Law that lets an AO carry liability itself. Then the seat shrinks further and the dashed box moves into the AO's bill.

3.3 Human needs by industry: the middle disappears, the need does not

Why this picture: nine needs, and why the bars are the widths they are
  • Why Max-Neef's nine. A closed list of fundamental needs that does not change with technology, so no need is skipped and no industry is invented. Maslow ranks; Max-Neef lists, which is what a market map wants.
  • Why the widths. Each bar is the mid-point of the range in 3.4, which comes from the three sorting questions: can a machine check it, does it need a body, must someone be liable.
  • Why care barely moves. Presence is a body in a place. Nothing in the three questions lets an AO take it.
  • Why creation is widest. Words and code are checkable, bodiless, and low in liability. Run 5 made a site from words with one stamp.
  • Evidence. Six of the nine rows have a Human Simulation goal file or a portfolio company behind them (right column of the table below).
  • What would change it. A row measured in rung 5 of the experiment. The bars are the only projection on this page drawn with a ruler; they are still a projection.

3.4 The share: three questions sort every piece of work

Why this picture: three questions, in this order
  • Claim. Three questions are enough to sort any piece of work between an AO and a human org, and their order matters.
  • Why these three. They are the three things the runtime already enforces or cannot do: engines check against closed lists (checkability); nothing in the app has a body (place); the owner stamps what goes out (liability).
  • Why this order. Work a machine cannot check never leaves a human, whatever else is true, so that question comes first. A body is a hard limit, so second. Liability is the softest: it can be shared at the seat, so last.
  • Why the sorting is not a share. It sorts pieces of work, not jobs. A dermatologist's day has pieces in all four boxes. The table under it turns the sort into a range per industry; that step is the projection.
  • What would change it. Robots that put a body in a place cheaply (the second question flips), or law that lets an AO be liable (the third question flips).

3.5 The next wants: each solved want creates the next

Solved: anything made of words or code is cheap.
Provenance. Who made this, a person or an AO? Humans decide what counts; AOs verify and sign.
Solved: there is more output than anyone can read.
Curation. Taste becomes a paid service. Human curators choose; AOs rank and filter.
Solved: many AOs act for me.
Trust and identity of AOs. Which AO speaks for whom? A registry and a reputation. Law sets it; the meta-AO runs it.
Solved: my AO deals with yours.
Fair trade and disputes. Escrow, arbitration, a ledger. Courts and insurers with AOs doing the bookkeeping.
Solved: my context is everywhere.
My context as mine. Portability, privacy, the right to take my AO elsewhere. Standards set by people, enforced by AOs.
Solved: hours are freed.
Meaning and the human-made premium. Craft, community, presence. Mostly human; AOs do the logistics around it.
Solved: everyone has an AO.
A better AO than others. Speed, compute, data. The AO's want for compute is the human's new bill, and a status race.
Solved: AOs act in the world.
Safety from AOs. Liability products, insurance, kill switches. Insurers and regulators with AOs doing the audit.
Why this ladder: each solved want creates the next, and two rungs are already visible
  • The rule. A solved want removes a scarcity and creates a new one next to it. Cheap output makes attention scarce (curation); many agents make identity scarce (trust); freed hours make meaning scarce. Jevons at the level of wants.
  • Why the pairs. Each next want has a human part (set the rule, choose, decide what counts) and an AO part (verify, rank, keep the ledger). That pairing is the founder's "AIs also have wants which other AIs plus humans are solving".
  • Evidence. Provenance already appeared as a stamped rule in run 3 ("Nothing goes on the site without a source line under it", finding 45; section 9, step 11). The AO's want for compute and the internet appeared as Identity's engine ask (N-024; section 9, step 3).
  • What would change it. If provenance is solved by the model vendors at the token level (signed outputs), the first rung moves from the AO layer to the model layer and the first next want is curation.

Projection Every next want needs a human to set the rule and an AO to keep the ledger. That pair is the civilization.

Lifecycle and transition HUMAN AND AGENT · how it moves

Three questions in one place: what kinds of AI get made along the lifecycle, from small to large, and where to cut that lifecycle; the small markets of agent orgs that make agent orgs; and how the economy gets from here to there, with the second- and third-order effects.

1. The AI lifecycle, and where to cut it

Cut by size and by stage; industry is the instance, not a cut

Every AI unit on Sutra lives one life: it is shaped from words, it runs a line of checked steps, it files what it made, it is judged against the brief and the rules, it grows (is promoted, generalised, reused) or is retired. That is Native's loop. The unit comes in six sizes, the six levels: atom, workflow, engine, department, agent org, meta-AO.

The founder's question was where to cut the problem so that the types can be defined. Three candidate lines were tested for the MECE property:

Candidate cutMutually exclusive?Collectively exhaustive?Verdict
By size (the six levels)yes: a unit has one levelyes: nothing sits outside the sixkeep
By lifecycle stage (shape, run, file, judge, grow)yes: a unit is at one stage at a time; a type does one stage's workyes: the loop has no other stagekeep
By industry scenario (health, food, clothing, ...)no: the same type appears in every industryno: industries overlap and multiplynot a cut; the instance dimension
Why this grid: why size times stage, and why industry is not a line
  • Claim. Every AI type is one cell of size times stage; industries are where the cells are instantiated, not a third axis.
  • Working. The MECE test in the table above: industry fails both halves (the same Audit type appears in a clinic and a tailor; industries overlap). Size and stage each pass, and their product covers every unit on Sutra with no cell shared.
  • Evidence. The bottom four rows are read off the running system: the five functions, the Record, the Library, engines born from words (run 5), closed-list checks (every run). The top rows are the next-to-build list of the model and the experiment's rungs 3 to 5.
  • Where not. The seat cell and the disputes cell: judgement at the agent org and above needs someone liable, so no AI type is built there.
  • What would change it. A unit that does not fit a cell (a type that spans two stages by design): then the stage axis needs a sixth column, not a new axis.

2. Pockets: markets of agent orgs that make agent orgs

A pocket is one cell sold to every agent org that needs it

The founder's idea: small pockets of agent-org markets, the "SaaS of AO", agent orgs whose product is making or serving other agent orgs, deployed to all of them, combining pairwise as the market needs. In the grid above a pocket is a cell offered as a service: an Adaptation pocket shapes lines for departments that have none; an Audit pocket judges filings for departments that want a second reader; a Source Reader pocket reaches the web for any department; a Publisher pocket puts anything on a host.

Why this picture: why pockets are cells, and why the pair count is the point
  • Claim. The "SaaS of AO" is a cell of the lifecycle grid sold across agent orgs; the pairings between pockets are too many to design and must be priced by a market.
  • Why cells. A pocket that spans stages is a department, not a pocket; a pocket that spans sizes is a platform. The grid keeps a pocket to one job, which is what makes it sellable to many buyers.
  • Why the pair count. N pockets give N(N-1)/2 pairs: 45 at ten, 19,900 at two hundred. No designer enumerates them; the registry (the meta-AO) records which pairs were bought and kept, and that is the pricing.
  • Evidence. Run 3: a Source Reader engine shaped for one department; run 5: a line of engines born on the fly. Both are pockets in waiting. Nothing yet sold across departments: rung 3.
  • What would change it. If pockets are cheaper to re-make than to buy (tokens cheaper than trust), there is no pocket market and every agent org makes its own.

3. The transition: from here to the settled state

The founder's assumptions, held

T1. The market structure does not change. The same industries serve the same needs; what changes is who does the hours inside them. Held as an assumption, not a finding.
T2. Human needs do not change significantly. The nine needs of the futures page hold through H2; wants change, needs do not.
T3. Markets speed up on existing technology. The first effect is speed in the markets that exist, not new markets.
T4. Industries with many humans and high cost get rejigged. Where the payroll is large and the work is checkable, the rejig is largest and earliest.

Four diffusion shapes, by the two things that set the pace

Two things set how an industry moves: whether its work is checkable by a machine (the sorting rule), and whether software already worked there. Subjective, non-deterministic work kept software out of many industries; agent orgs handle that subjectivity, so those industries do not slide, they leap.

ShapeIndustry classWhy that shapeExamplesWhat it feels like
Leapsoftware never worked: the work was subjective, the firm informalno incumbent system to replace; the agent org is the first officetailors, small clinics, local services, farmsa jump straight to agent-org-native, with little chaos because there was nothing to unlearn

Stallbody and liability boundthe sorting questions keep the hours human; only the office around them movesconstruction, nursing, cooking, driving until robotsthe office changes, the floor does not
Why these curves: two causes, four shapes, and why H1 sits at the jerk
  • Claim. Diffusion is not one curve. Two causes, checkability and whether software already worked, give four shapes, and the horizons are read off the shapes, not off a calendar.
  • Why the leap is first. No incumbent system, no org chart to unlearn, and the agent org handles the subjectivity that kept software out. The Human Simulation runs are all leaps: a tailor, a farm, a clinic listing, a trip.
  • Why the jerk wobbles. Incumbents move in a rush, find that the technical part is easy and the organisational part (who holds the seat, what the manager does now) is not, and stall before they settle. That is the chaos the founder named.
  • Why H1 and H2. Calendar years date a page the first time a shock moves the curve. H1 is defined as the jerk's peak, H2 as the flattening; the hub says what they mean in years today.
  • What would change it. Regulation that freezes the jerk industries (then jerk becomes stall), or robots that free the stall (then stall becomes a late slide).

Projection So what: the transition is four different stories told at once. The jerk is where the pain and the headlines are; the leap is where the first agent orgs are sold; the stall is where nothing much happens for a decade. Sutra's niche, the website department, sits in the leap.

Where organizations on Sutra are going

Most companies do not have a headcount problem or an intelligence problem. They have a structure problem. A fifty-person company outruns fifty talented freelancers because work gets sorted before anyone touches it, each department knows what it exists to do, nothing ships until it is checked, and there is a record of what happened. Sutra’s bet: once that structure is software, the size of what one person can run stops being a function of who they can hire.

That paragraph is our thesis, not an observed law — we think work moves this way when structure becomes software. Everything below says plainly which parts are running today, which are evidence from simulation, and which are still ahead.

The five chapters

The product grows the way it says companies should grow: each chapter must prove itself in the record before the next one starts. No chapter ships on promises — each has a completion proof you could check yourself.

CHAPTER 1Your recurring work runs written and checked

Name one thing your company does again and again. Sutra does it with you once, writes down what actually happened, and offers it back as a workflow you approve. From then on that job runs the written way, with a check it cannot skip — and every run is recorded. Nothing is improvised twice.

Done when — the same job has run 20 times through its written form, the check visibly passing, with zero improvised repeats. Read straight off the record, not off a dashboard.
CHAPTER 2The company gets measurably better at its own work

Because every run was recorded, the system now knows what the work actually costs and where it stumbles. It gives honest arrival times ("Tuesday — based on the last 21 runs"), schedules backwards from your deadlines, and earns the right to stop asking permission for steps it has never gotten wrong.

Done when — interruptions on the same work are cut in half ("it asked me 12 times last month, 4 times this month") and every estimate cites the runs it came from.
CHAPTER 3Departments trade work without a human carrying it

Two parts of the company exchange work directly: a request lands, joins the receiving side’s own queue on its own priorities, and comes back with evidence attached. Nobody walks anything down a hallway; nothing gets lost between teams.

Done when — one exchange completes brokered by nobody, traceable end to end under a single thread.
CHAPTER 4A whole effort runs through the system — and you can reconstruct it

A real launch — product, campaign, filing — runs end to end: many departments, one record. Afterward you can replay exactly what happened, who decided what, and why. Recurring cross-team problems get a standing owner instead of a hallway conversation.

Done when — one real launch completes this way, and the owner is interrupted fewer times than our simulated benchmark (19 — see the evidence section below).
CHAPTER 5It installs anywhere and grows on its own record

A stranger’s company: the system interviews them, sets up a skeleton shaped to their work, and grows from their own record the way it grew from ours. Playbooks travel between companies; a company’s data never does.

Done when — one install on a computer we never touched, running and improving on its own record.

Where we are today

QuestionHonest answer
Current chapterBuilding chapter 1. The design decisions that unlock it were closed in a founder sitting concluded 2026-08-17 (~30 rulings; 4 of them gate chapter 1).
Proof achieved so farThe honest task runner at the core of chapter 1 already runs our own operation: 202 recorded work rows, each carrying its goal and the check that closed it. Roughly 80% of chapter 1’s machinery exists; the piece being built now is the workflow registry itself.
Next evidence thresholdThe first registered workflow followed 20 times with its check passing — chapter 1’s exit.
Who runs it todayOne operator runs six companies’ operations through this system. That is a load test, not a customer count — the claim is not that AI replaces a team; it is that a company can be small and still be properly run.

Why we believe structure is the lever — the evidence and its limits

Before committing to chapters 3 and 4 we simulated the same six operating scenarios (a lending company: collections, compliance, launches) under two organizational shapes: ad-hoc coordination for every crossing, versus standing owners plus written agreements between departments.

MeasureAd-hoc shapeStructured shape
Times the founder was interrupted4519
New coordination objects invented2515
Could you reconstruct what happened? (1-5)2.34.0
Scenarios won06 of 6
What this does and does not show. These are results from a paper simulation, not from live companies — they suggest structure pays most on recurring cross-team work and least on genuine novelty, and they set the benchmark chapter 4 must beat in reality (fewer than 19 interruptions on a real launch). They are design evidence, not market evidence.

Why there are no dates on this page

We do not forecast dates for chapters. We publish the proof required for each chapter and the proof achieved so far — the table above. A chapter that shipped on a date instead of on its evidence would be the exact failure this system exists to prevent. The operating cadence is real (the ledger grows daily, and the design queue is decided in recorded sittings); the calendar commitments are deliberately absent.

What we do not know yet

  • Pricing. Three customer sizes are sketched; price points are deliberately unset until operating-economics data exists. The plugin itself is free and MIT-licensed.
  • External adoption. No outside customer is claimed on this page. The six-company operation is ours.
  • Chapters 3-5 in detail. Their contents are provisional by design — they get re-cut when chapter 1’s record starts talking. That is the evidence-gating applied to the roadmap itself.

From capabilities to a solution A READING · JUDGED, NOT YET TESTED

A list of capabilities does not tell you what to build. Departments are one solution, not the answer. Engineers bridge the gap with conceptual design: group the capabilities into the jobs the system must do, list several different ways to do each job, combine them into whole solutions that really differ, and score every solution against every capability. Only then is one chosen and elaborated. Here that was run on the 15 capabilities ruled now: 5 solutions, three judges, one refuter.

Founder's question, 2026-09-27: the department structure is a solution; from the capabilities, how did we land on departments or any other solution; what is the process from here to there? Until this page, the site assumed departments (they came into Core from the org-design work and Beer's Viable System Model, not from these capabilities).

1 · The process

2 · The jobs, and the ways each could be done

3 · The 5 solutions

A · Departments

like A traditional company org chart: Collections, Underwriting and Finance teams, each with a head, a charter and a to-do list.

The company is a tree of departments. Each department has a charter that states its goal, rules and caps, and it keeps its own shelf of documents and its own run book. The five functions (Identity, Adaptation, Priority, Coordination, Audit) are standing jobs in every department. Work items live inside a department, inherit its goal and checks, and start from its calendar or inbox. Anything the charter does not cover goes to the department head. This is closest to Core today, so it is the cheapest to build, and a founder can read the org chart at a glance. It grows from one step as a department of one. The cost is that a department owns each document, so work that crosses departments needs asks, and a single piece of work cannot exist without a department around it.

The lending company: Collections owns the overdue-loans list and runs a daily reminder routine, and Underwriting owns credit decisions. A write-off above the charter's limit waits for the Collections head to approve it.

B · Flat pipeline

like A spreadsheet of formulas: each cell recalculates when the cells it reads change, and there are no teams.

There is no grouping. The company is one shared ledger of documents and a flat list of pieces of work. Each piece of work carries its own card: its goal, its check, the documents it reads and writes, its clock and wake-ups, its rules and its cap. When a document gets a new version, the work that reads it runs, and the chain brake counts the steps. One company journal records everything. It starts from one step with nothing extra, and every rule sits next to the work it governs. The cost is that at a hundred pieces of work the founder faces a long list with no shape, and the same rule gets copied onto many cards.

The lending company: A new loan application wakes the credit-score work. Its score document wakes the decision work, and the approval wakes the offer-letter work. Each card carries its own cap and rules, and nothing is named Underwriting.

C · Goal projects

like A consulting engagement or a film crew: a team forms around one goal, works from one case file, and disbands when the goal is met.

Work is grouped by goal, not by function. A project opens on request with a brief (goal, checks, rules, caps and a lead) and a case file of documents. Inside the project, each step wakes when its input arrives. When the goal is met, the case file is sealed and archived with its history, and the project dissolves. Nothing sits idle, and every document sits next to the reason it exists. The weak spot is standing work. The daily collections run and the monthly close have no natural home, so each becomes a project that never ends, which is a department under another name. Reading across many closed case files is also hard.

The lending company: Each large loan is a project. Its case file holds the application, the credit memo and the approval, the underwriting lead approves exceptions, and the file is sealed at disbursal. The monthly close is a project reopened every month.

D · Open task board

like A freelance marketplace or a taxi dispatch queue: work is posted, and whoever is free and qualified picks it up.

There is one shared board. Every trigger (a request, a clock tick or a new version) posts a ticket with a goal, an acceptance check and a spend limit. Any person or AI that the company rulebook allows can claim a ticket. They read and write documents in the shared ledger and close the ticket by passing its check. Every ask and every undo goes to one approvals desk. Load balances well, and nothing has to be designed up front. The costs are that no one owns an outcome over time, the single desk becomes a bottleneck as the company grows, and the founder sees a queue rather than a company.

The lending company: The overnight tick posts 400 'chase overdue loan' tickets, and collection AIs and agents claim them. A 'waive late fee' ask lands on the shared desk, where a finance lead approves it.

E · Documents own themselves

like A land registry, or a protected Wikipedia page: the record itself says who may change it, how, and what needs a sign-off.

The documents are the company. Each document carries its own rules: who may add a version, what is refused, what needs approval, how hard an undo is, what a good version must pass, and what any run that writes it may spend. It keeps its own history and a list of the work to wake when it changes. Work is simply whatever adds a version, and it must satisfy the document it writes. This makes documents first-class and safe by default. The costs are that rules scatter across thousands of documents, it is hard to see who does what, and a goal bigger than one document has nowhere to live.

The lending company: The loan book document says only the disbursal work may add rows and that write-offs over a limit need Finance to approve them. Each new version wakes the collections-priority list, and each borrower's file carries its own undo rules.

4 · The scores

A Pugh matrix: solution A (Departments, what Core has today) is the datum. Every other solution is scored against it on every capability and every quality: better (+1), the same (0) or worse (-1). Three judges scored independently (the everyday user, safety and control, what can be built and scaled); a cell shows the majority.

JudgePickedWhy
userHybrid starting from A (Departments): each department's work items carry B's card (named reads and writes, and waking on a new version), and each document carries E's own rules (deny, the check on a good version, undo severity).Net scores against A: E +1, B -1, C -2, D -6. E and B win on capabilities but lose on legibility, scale and cost, which are what a founder and the teams feel every day. A keeps the org chart they can read at a glance and reuses Core, and it takes its weak spots from the others: B's explicit work cards for wiring, and E's rules on the document so the loan book refuses bad writes whichever department's work touches it. Cross-department reads are ruled LATER anyway, so A's main cost does not show up in the first build.
safetyHybrid starting from A (Departments), adding E's document-level guardsOn safety, E has the best net score (+4 against A's 0): refuse, approve, undo severity and checks all sit on the document at risk, so no work can slip past them. Its gaps are exactly A's strengths: a home for goals and schedules, a standing head to approve asks, a readable tree, and reuse of Core. So I would start from Departments and let each document on a department's shelf carry its own deny, stamp, undo and check rules. Per-run caps and the chain brake stay with the department's work.
buildHybrid starting from A (Departments), with B's per-work cards and a single company journal inside each department, and E's deny, undo-severity and check rules attached to key documents such as the loan book.A is the only option that reuses Core's org tree, charters, routines and asks, and the only one that stays readable at 100 units, because departments nest while flat lists, queues and scattered document rules do not. Its real gaps are waking on a new version, lineage across teams, and rules that sit on the record. B's wake-on-version cards with one journal close the first two, and E's rules on a few high-stakes documents close the third, all without giving up the org chart the founder can read.

5 · The choice, and its strongest counter

Leading: E · Documents own themselves (+1 against the datum). Change the lead: keep departments as what the founder sees and as the source of default rules, but build on B's ledger and work cards with E's rules on each document, because none of the 15 NOW capabilities needs a department and the tied scores mean A led only on a cost saving that is overstated.

What all three sources agree on: a hybrid. The three judges each start from Departments and borrow the flat pipeline's work cards and the rules that documents carry. The refuter keeps the same parts but puts them the other way up: one company ledger of documents, each with its own rules, and work cards underneath; departments on top, as how people see the company and where default rules come from. The majority scores side with the refuter: documents that own their rules lead, departments are the datum.

Recommended: documents underneath, departments on top. Build the base as a ledger of documents with their own rules and work cards (E with B); keep departments as the view and the source of default rules (A). None of the fifteen capabilities ruled now needs a department; people still need one to see the company.

Where the leader is weakest: Q6 documents come first: in A the department is the main thing and a document is just something on its shelf. That turns the ruled model around. The ruling says a company is documents plus the work between them, not teams that happen to own documents., Q3 grows from one step: the smallest thing A allows is a department with a charter and five standing jobs. The ruling asks for one input, one piece of work and one output. A single piece of work cannot exist in A unless a department is built around it first., 13 Wake on a new version: A starts work only from a calendar or an inbox. Starting work because a document changed is not part of A at all. All three judges had to borrow it from B., 14 Chain brake: real chains cross teams. A write-off in Collections adds a Finance ledger version, which wakes the Collections priority list. A brake kept inside each department cannot see the whole chain, so the same work can run twice or a loop can go unnoticed., 11 Lineage: when Finance's disbursal version is made from Underwriting's credit decision, the trail from input to output breaks at the line between the two shelves., 8 Ask and stamp: A sends everything the charter does not cover to the department head. The ruling says the person asked can be anyone: a person, an AI, a rule or another department. In A the head becomes the one bottleneck, the same problem D has with its single approvals desk., 10 Deny, 15 Undo severity and Q2 safe by default: rules written in a charter protect the team, not the document. The loan book is touched by work from Underwriting, Finance and Collections, so three charters guard it and they can disagree. Judge 3's fix puts rules on key documents only, which leaves every other document unguarded., Q5 cheap from what exists: A's cost advantage is smaller than it looks. Core has no store of documents with versions and history, and that store is the heart of the model. It has to be built new whichever option wins, so A only saves on the parts that were already cheap..

The strongest counter: Departments were not derived from the capabilities. They came from Core, and the roadmap was then written in their terms, so the scoring went in a circle. The NEXT list is titled "the first department grows", and "cross-department reads" was pushed to LATER. That is why the judges could say A's main cost "does not show up in the first build". Read the 15 NOW capabilities on their own and none of them needs a department. Every one belongs to a document (versions, lineage, deny, check, undo), to a piece of work (goal, named reads and writes, run log, cap, schedule, wake-up) or to a chain of work (the brake). The Pugh totals tied at 0 for all five options, and against A, E came out +1. So A won on cheapness and legibility, not on capabilities, and the cheapness is overstated because the versioned document store has to be built new anyway. Departments are the answer to Q1 (legible) and Q4 (scales), and as a grouping they are right. What is wrong is letting a department own documents. In lending, one credit decision is read on day one by disbursal (Finance) and by risk ranking (Collections), so crossing departments is everyday work, not a LATER case.

The hybrid that answers it: Flip which layer sits underneath. The base is B's single company ledger: every document there is versioned and carries E's own rules (who may add a version, what is refused, what a good version must pass, who signs off, how hard an undo is). Work is a set of B cards with named reads and writes, wake-on-version, a cap per run, and one company journal. One chain id and step count run across the whole company. A's departments sit on top as the grouping the founder reads. A department is a named folder of work cards plus the defaults those cards inherit (goal, caps, schedule) and a default person who approves. It never owns a document. Core carries over: a charter becomes the inherited defaults, a routine becomes a card with a clock, and an ask becomes a sign-off. Lending example: the loan book is one ledger document whose rules say only disbursal work may add rows and a write-off above the limit needs Finance's sign-off. Collections is a folder holding the daily-reminder card and the priority-list card, and both read the loan book. One brake counts the chain as it moves from Collections to Finance and back. Growth follows the ruled model: the start is one document, one card and one output, and a department folder appears only when a second card needs the same defaults. That keeps the org chart and fixes A's problems with crossing teams, lineage, the brake, and documents coming first.

6 · The process, as a skill

#StepDone when
1Group the capabilities into jobsevery capability sits in exactly one job
2List the ways for each job3 or 4 genuinely different ways per job, in plain words
3Combine ways into whole solutionsat least four solutions that differ in shape, today's among them
4Score against every capability and qualitya Pugh matrix with today's solution as the datum, several independent judges, the majority per cell
5Attack the leadera refuter names its weakest rows and a hybrid that answers them
6Choose, then elaboratea dated ruling; the chosen solution goes to the operable design and a walk

The fields behind it: systematic design (Pahl and Beitz: function structure, working principles, concept variants, evaluation); the morphological box (Fritz Zwicky), which lays out the ways per job and finds solutions as paths through it; and Pugh's controlled convergence, which scores concepts against a datum and merges the strong ideas of the losers into the winner. Confidence: high on the fields; the scores here are model judgement, rung 1 to 2, until users and a build test them.

Inside the Product Vertical TWO ENGINES · DEFINED

The Era-4 picture on The Product Journey drew stations named “pricing” and “check.” Honest admission: those stations were never defined anywhere. This page opens them up — defines both properly, in the system’s own words — and answers the bigger question: when a department matures, does it become one big engine, or a cascade of smaller systems?

1 · The cascade — how one department becomes many systems

The model already contains the answer, and it costs nothing extra. A System is a composition of engines: work engines that do the domain’s jobs, plus at least one glue engine (coordination, priority, audit, adaptation, identity) that runs above the work engines and is owned by none of them. And the model says recursion is free: a System viewed from outside is just one bigger Engine.

So the cascade is not a new mechanism. It is the same shape at every level:

Fig 1 · The Product department as a System — and one of its engines opened up, showing the same shape one level down.

The precise rule (this matters): a work engine does not become a child System by “getting bigger.” It becomes a child System exactly when it splits into sub-engines and at least one glue engine runs above them that none of the sub-engines owns. That is the System test, applied one level down. Maturity per level is just “which glue engines exist yet” — the S1→S5 ladder, applied at every level of the cascade independently.
Glue engines are not plumbing. They are first-class: each has its own charter slice, its own record, its own verify. The department’s audit engine, for example, is what feeds the roll-up board in §5. If you cannot point at the glue engine that arbitrates between two work engines, you do not have a System — you have a pipeline.

2 · From drawn station to real engine

The two stations we are about to define lived as habits: a written procedure someone follows, with results scattered in tools. The model has names for each stage of becoming real — and this page needs one name the sheet does not have yet.

StageWhat it isWhat it has
Written WorkflowThe procedure, written down. W-price-loan starts here.Steps + verify per atom. No memory of its own runs.
EngineThe grown thing: procedure + its accumulated record slice + adaptation.Everything above, plus: its own record of decisions + outcomes, dials per node, a declared read surface, continuous conformance checks.
Child SystemThe engine, split into sub-engines with unowned glue above them.Everything above, plus: its own glue engines. The cascade’s next level.
New term (proposed, routed to the decision sheet as A21): Engine Formation — the act by which a written Workflow, having accumulated enough record slice to support adaptation, becomes an Engine. It is distinct from two neighbors it is often confused with: crystallization (the per-node dial that moves one step from LLM to code) and template promotion (D38’s L2→L1→L0 ladder, which distributes a template to the fleet). Formation is about one org’s workflow growing up; crystallization is about one node hardening; promotion is about a template travelling. Alternatives recorded for the founder’s sitting: Engine Accretion (both peer AIs offered it — foregrounds the record-slice mechanism), Engine Emergence, Hardening (rejected: sounds final; engines keep adapting).

The human lever. One call stays human: where the acceptable-risk line sits. The engine brings the evidence (cohort curves, premium math, competitor bands) and proposes; the person sets the threshold and owns it. This is the same tradeoff threshold pattern as every other department’s lever — goals, judgment, taste concentrated at one declared seat, not smeared through the pipeline.

Running problems, on the quadrant board (per-engine board — the department roll-up is §5):

Known-known · routine fixCost-of-funds feed lags two days, so premiums go stale every month-end. Known problem, known fix — a written workflow handles the refresh; boring on purpose. Known-unknown · data gapThe new-to-credit segment’s cohort is too small — the premium there is a guess with wide bands. We know exactly what we don’t know; only more repayment history closes it. Not a judgment problem, a data problem.
Unknown-known · shadow practiceAn operator prices gold-loan top-ups in a side spreadsheet with a feel-based discount. The company knows how (a human does it daily) — the system doesn’t. Classic shadow system: the discount logic must be pulled onto the record before it walks out the door with the operator. Unknown-unknown · novel shockA market event reshapes default behavior across every cohort at once — no template fits, past records mislead. The engine cannot price this; the escalation contract fires, the human seat + the written decision procedure take over, and run one becomes the first record of the new pattern.

The human lever. Nearly nothing — and that is the design. One call stays human: when a new regulation lands, a person interprets it before it becomes an encoded rule. The tradeoff threshold here is interpretive: how conservatively to read an ambiguous rule until a clarification arrives. Everything after interpretation is frozen.

Running problems, on the quadrant board:

Known-known · routine fixRule-register version drift between the test and live environments — caught by the version check, fixed by a written sync workflow. Boring on purpose. Known-unknown · data gapA new regulatory circular has landed and is being interpreted — its impact on in-flight loans is known-unknown until the human seat finishes reading and the rule is encoded.
Unknown-known · shadow practiceThe ops team quietly applies an extra informal check (“we always call the employer for this segment”) that lives in no register. A human knows it; the system doesn’t. It must be pulled onto the record — encoded or explicitly rejected. Unknown-unknown · novel shockA fraud pattern that passes every encoded check. No rule catches it because no rule imagined it. First detection comes from downstream evidence (Collections anomalies), escalates to human + decision procedure, and the miss becomes the audit finding that becomes the next rule.

5 · The department roll-up board

Each engine keeps its own quadrant board — that is the primary lens, because problems have engine-local texture. But the cascade demands one more: the department System (itself one bigger engine from outside) runs an aggregate board, owned by its glue — the audit + adaptation engines — and fed by every engine board below.

What the roll-up catchesExample
Shocks that span enginesThe market event in pricing’s unknown-unknown cell also floods comply-check’s exception lane — one shock, two local boards; only the roll-up sees it is the same shock.
Shadow practices that span enginesThe same operator’s side spreadsheet feeds both a pricing discount and an informal check — one shadow system, visible only in aggregate.
Systemic driftThree engines each slightly out of conformance is a department problem, not three engine problems.

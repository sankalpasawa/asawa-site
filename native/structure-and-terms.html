How Sutra is organized — the structure and the words FRONT DOOR · zoom 1

Seven words carry the whole organization. A Domain (you say "department") owns Charters, receives Placements, and produces Artifacts. Inside Artifact sit three refinements: Module, Component, and doc layer. Everything the system creates fits these dimensions; a genuinely new kind is flagged to you, never added silently. Green = ruled in canon. Blue = founder direction of 2026-09-11. Amber = proposed, awaiting ruling.

The structure

The tree is the same at every level: a sub-department is a child Domain with its own charters, placements and artifacts. Deeper nodes never rearrange when status changes; only the counts move.

The rule for anything new FOUNDER DIRECTION 2026-09-11

  • Closed vocabulary. Every output the system creates is a Domain, a Charter, a Placement, or an Artifact (with its kind, facing and doc layer; possibly a Module or Component). Nothing else.
  • Complete creation. When something new is created, all its dimensions are created with it: the domain it belongs to, the charter it answers to, the placement that pins it, and the typed artifact record. A subagent checks this on every creation; it cannot be bypassed.
  • New kinds are events. If an output does not fit any dimension, the system says so to you as a highlighted event. It never adds a kind silently.

Implementation is the directory program (paper first: the complete directory of everything that exists; then the creation guard; the registry sync and the Mac app view come after).

The six layers folded from the-six-layers.html on 2026-09-29

Sutra helps you design your work, then runs it. It does that in six layers. The first three are the spine: know the company, design the work, put agents on it. The other three keep it honest and growing: feedback, marketplaces, tooling.

In one sentence: Sutra builds a model of the organisation, designs workflows on top of that model, and deploys agents to execute those workflows — then records what happened and improves both, with your approval.

The six layers in one picture

Green is the spine. Grey is what keeps it honest and growing.

Each layer, with an example

All examples are one company: a digital lender that takes applications, sanctions loans, disburses money and collects EMIs. Its rule: a disbursal above ₹5 lakh needs a second approval.

1 · Ontology
What exists in this company, and how is it connected?
Applicants, credit officers, loans, EMIs. A credit officer owns an application. An application can become a sanctioned loan. A disbursal above ₹5 lakh needs a second approval.
departments with written purposes, and a read-back before work starts: “this is a Finance request, medium size, owned by Collections”.
2 · Workflow design
How should this outcome be produced?
Verify identity → score → sanction or decline → disburse → reconcile the first EMI. If identity cannot be verified, stop and ask the owner. Done means: money moved, ledger matches, applicant informed.
a playbook with steps and a declared “done means this” check.
3 · Agents
Who or what does the work?
The agent runs the identity check, the scoring and the sanction on its own, then pauses before any step that needs an approval — such as a disbursal above ₹5 lakh.
steps ticking by, a receipt at the end, and a question only when the decision is genuinely yours.
4 · Feedback
How does it get better?
The bank file arrived late three Fridays running, and the check failed each time. The playbook gains a “wait until 11:00 on Fridays” step — after you approve it.
“Want me to keep this as the way we do it?” Every run is recorded. Sutra checks whether a playbook still works and flags it when it stops passing.
5 · Marketplaces
Where do playbooks come from?
Your identity-check playbook started as a shared template. You kept the shape. Your limits, your rules and your customers stayed yours.
playbook shapes can travel between companies; your customers, numbers and decisions do not.
6 · Tooling
What can the agent touch and change?
The bank export, the ledger, the CRM, the message to the applicant.
connectors to those systems, with every action written to the record.

One request, through all six

You type: “disburse loan 4821”. Here is what happens, in order.

Layer 1 · Ontology
Loan 4821 is a sanctioned loan of ₹7 lakh, owned by credit officer Meera. Her limit is ₹5 lakh, so a second approval is required. The company model already knows all of this.
Layer 2 · Workflow design
The disbursal playbook applies: confirm sanction → second approval → send bank instruction → post to ledger → inform applicant. Done means: money moved, ledger matches, applicant told.
Layer 5 · Marketplaces
That playbook’s shape came from a shared lending template last quarter. The approval limit in it is yours.
Layer 6 · Tooling
The agent can reach the bank portal, the ledger and the messaging channel. Nothing else.
Layer 3 · Agents
The agent confirms the sanction, then stops: “₹7 lakh is above Meera’s limit. Approve?” You approve. It sends the instruction, posts the ledger entry, messages the applicant, and hands you a receipt.
Layer 4 · Feedback
The run is recorded and the check passed. If it had failed, work would have stopped with the evidence attached. If you had changed a step, Sutra would ask: “keep this as the way we do it?”

Numbers are the order in time. Marketplaces and tooling equip the agent before it runs.

Ontology is not workflow

The map does not change when you reorder the route. The route means nothing without the map.

This is why the order matters. A workflow without an ontology has nobody to own it, nothing to check against, and nowhere to put an exception. An agent without a workflow improvises. Sutra insists on the model first, the design second, the agent third.

The loop is the product

Agents do the work, and they are not what makes Sutra different. What compounds is the loop: understand the company, design its work, run it, record what came back, correct the model and the playbooks. Take the feedback layer away and you have a collection of agents. Keep it and you have a system whose work can be inspected, corrected and reused.
In plain words: understand the organisation, design its work, execute its work. Tooling lets the agents act; feedback lets the system improve. (The short form: Ontology → Orchestration → Agency.)

What comes first: the six families, ranked RULED BY MAJORITY · FOUNDER MAY VETO

Every capability of the six families, 43 in all, ranked into three tiers: now (15), next (17) and later (11). The order inside each tier is the build order. The system diagram is grown in exactly this order: the system, grown in this order.

Founder's direction, 2026-09-26: before growing the system diagram, rank all the features and capabilities of the six families, which come first and which later, and publish that. Decided under D79 (rulings by veto): three independent judges, two refuters, the majority in code, a reversal trigger; the ruling stands unless the founder vetoes it.

3 · How it was decided

JudgeLensPrinciple, in its own words
valuevalue to the first userNow holds only what gets one loan-book-to-promise loop running safely with borrowers (versioned artifacts, checked runs, stamped capped calls, compliance denials) plus the read-back that shows promises moved; everything that needs a multi-piece graph, accumulated history, or a second department waits.
safetysafety before the worldBuild now whatever stands between an error and a borrower, money or the outside world (a check, a stamp, a refusal, lineage, caps and a trace), and hold every automatic chain until its brake ships with it.
buildbuild order and dependenciesBuild strictly up the dependency graph from the artifact contract (E1, G2) through goal, check, run log and lineage to a stamped, capped, deny-guarded call to the world. Ship each trigger with its brake. Everything that needs accumulated record, several pieces or several departments waits for the next or later tier.

Amended by the founder, 2026-09-26

The ruling stood unless vetoed; the founder amended it:

  • The world: now → later. the world is a use case of the architecture, like a tool built on the product; focus on it later
  • Wake on a new version stays now: wake on a new version is very important

Redefined

  • Run on request: anyone can start a run: a person, an AI, a rule, another piece of work
  • Check: the output must be verifiable; the shape of the work picks which kind of check
  • Ask and stamp: an action the rules do not cover waits for an answer (approve, change, decline) from whoever is asked: a person, an AI, a rule or another department
  • Cap per run: a limit on what one run may spend, per resource dimension: tokens, money, calls, time
  • Undo, with severity: every action inside the ecosystem names how it is undone, and a severity that sets who must approve the undo

How the ruling was reached. Three judges ranked all 43 capabilities independently. A capability lands in a tier when two of the three put it there; where all three disagreed (none), it lands in next. Two refuters then attacked the majority: one for too much in now, one for too little. A strong move was applied when the two refuters did not contradict each other. Last, code enforced the dependencies: nothing is built before what it needs.

Moves the refuters won

  • Lineage gate: now → next. over-scope: V4 re-asks an outside action that would otherwise run on its own. In the first 6 weeks there is no such action: V2 has a person stamp every call batch, and V5 (Rules from stamps, the first thing that turns an ask into a standing rule) is already in next. So V4 has nothing to gate. What the department does instead: the V2 ask card shows each input's version and its S2 check result, for example 'Plan v12 from Due list v31, check passed'. The person declines if any input is unchecked or failed. No harm follows because a person sees every outside action. The condition is that V4 must ship with or before V5.
  • Recipe as an artifact: now → next. over-scope: Nothing in now depends on G8; its first consumer is G5 (Growth proposal), which is in next. What the department does instead: each recipe (goal, check, cap, schedule) stays a file under version control. The G1 run log records the recipe's commit id as its version, for example 'Intake ran 09:02 on recipe a1b2c3', and a recipe change is a normal reviewed commit by the builder. A small team makes only a few recipe changes in 6 weeks, and the commit history is the full audit trail. No harm follows because every run still names the exact recipe it used. What waits is only the in-product flow where a change is a decision that writes recipe v7, and that flow is only needed once G5 starts proposing changes.
  • Undo, with severity: next → now. under-scope: E7 puts real calls to real borrowers into NOW, but no outside action names how it is undone. Failure in weeks 2-4: a borrower pays at 10:30, Call borrower is working from a plan stamped at 09:30 on Loan book v412, and the borrower is called at 11:00. There is no apology SMS and no loan note, and the remedy is not recorded. The complaint arrives, and the team cannot show what was done or undo it the same way next time. Its needs, V2 and E7, are both in NOW, so this adds a declared field on every outside-acting recipe (G8), not a new subsystem.
  • Chain brake: next → now. under-scope: The 'no piece runs twice for one root' half is needed without T3. Failure in week 1 or 2: the scheduled Call borrower run fails after 30 of its 60 calls, and someone presses Run now (T1) on the same Outreach plan v12. V4 does not ask again, because the plan is not unchecked, failed or superseded, and R1 caps each run, not each root. So the same 30 borrowers are called twice in one day, which is harassment and a contact-frequency breach. A once-per-root key on outside actions stops it. The hop limit can wait for T3 in NEXT.

Moved up because something earlier needs them

  • Wake on a new version: next → now, because Chain brake needs it

Dissent on record, not applied

  • Lineage to next (over-scope, weak): With V4 moved to next, no item in now needs G3. What the department does instead: the E1 contract writes every read and write to the G1 run log with version ids, and the S2 result goes into the same log entry. To answer 'which due list version produced this call plan, and did it pass its check', you join the log. No harm follows at first-department volume: tracing a wrong call is slower but still exact. It is weak because in a regulated lending audit a stored lineage record is easier to defend than a join, and building G3 costs little more than saving that join. If the team fears a regulator's question in weeks 1-6, keep G3 in now. (weak; recorded as dissent)
  • Branch to now (under-scope, weak): E7 turns replies into artifacts in NOW, but the skeleton has only Record promise to read them. A reply of 'stop calling me' or 'I dispute this debt' is not a promise, so nothing picks it up. The next daily plan calls the borrower again, which breaks a cease or dispute request within weeks. Branching on reply kind (record it or stop and escalate) closes this, and it needs only E1. Weak because Record promise could instead be widened by hand to also write a do-not-contact list that V3 reads. (weak; recorded as dissent)
  • Case file to now (under-scope, weak): The due list is rebuilt daily from the loan book, so a still-late loan is due again every day. A contact-frequency deny (V3, e.g. at most N attempts per borrower in 7 days) needs per-loan attempt history that outlives one run. Without it, the daily schedule (T2) can call the same borrower 7 days running in week 1. Its needs, E1 and G2, are both in NOW. Weak because Plan outreach could rebuild the history from the Borrower reply artifacts and run logs instead. (weak; recorded as dissent)
  • Must-cohere to now (under-scope, weak): The promise ledger is the department's key output, and it has two writers: engine calls and people's own calls. Without an artifact-level rule of one live promise per loan, two conflicting promises can both be live. The due list then drops the loan while one of them is already broken, so the borrower goes unchased, or gets chased despite a live promise. Its need, G2, is in NOW. Weak because an S2 check on each writer's recipe can hold the same rule at first. (weak; recorded as dissent)

The question: in the system, what shape should a company take? Five approaches were compared against every capability ruled now. The recommendation: documents that carry their own rules, joined by work cards. No org structure is needed.

1 · Five approaches

A · Departments

like an org chart

teams, each with its own documents, rules and a head
+ familiar: everyone reads an org chart
so: the founder finds any team in seconds; nobody needs it explained
+ Core already has it
so: the least new code: the org tree, charters, routines and asks exist
− a document belongs to one team
so: the loan book is used by underwriting, collections and finance: two of them ask every time, and three rule sets can disagree
− whatever the rules do not cover waits on the head
so: the head becomes a queue; work stalls when they are busy
− cannot start from one step
so: even one piece of work needs a whole department, with a charter and five functions, first
0 (reference)

B · Flat pipeline

like a spreadsheet of formulas

documents and work cards, with no grouping
+ starts from one step
so: the first piece of work runs on day one, with nothing around it
+ work wakes when its input changes
so: nobody schedules or chases: a new loan book reaches the due list by itself
− a long list with no shape at a hundred pieces
so: the founder cannot see the company; finding who owns what means searching
− the same rule copied onto many cards
so: change a rule once and miss it on ten cards
-1
part of the recommendation

C · Goal projects

like a film crew

a team forms around one goal, then disbands
+ every document sits beside its reason
so: anyone can see why a document exists
+ nothing sits idle
so: a team exists only while there is a goal: no standing cost
− daily, standing work has no home
so: the daily collections run becomes a project that never ends: a department under another name
− history is sealed in closed case files
so: learning across projects means opening many closed files
-2

D · Open task board

like a freelance marketplace

work is posted; anyone allowed picks it up
+ spreads the load
so: whoever is free takes the next task; nobody waits on a team
+ anyone allowed can pick up work
so: AIs and people are interchangeable for a task
− nobody owns the outcome
so: tasks get done, but nobody answers for promises kept this week
− one approvals desk for everything
so: the bottleneck moves to the desk; it does not go away
− rules must be company-wide
so: a risky task looks like any other on the board
-4

E · Documents own themselves

like a land registry

each document carries its own rules
+ safety sits on the thing at risk
so: no work can change the loan book without passing the loan book's own rules, whoever runs it
+ full history on every version
so: any number can be traced back to what made it
− rules are scattered across documents
so: with thousands of documents there is no single rulebook to read
− nowhere for goals and schedules
so: a document knows what it must be, not what work should happen or when
+1
part of the recommendation

3 · The 5 solutions

A · Departments

like A traditional company org chart: Collections, Underwriting and Finance teams, each with a head, a charter and a to-do list.

The company is a tree of departments. Each department has a charter that states its goal, rules and caps, and it keeps its own shelf of documents and its own run book. The five functions (Identity, Adaptation, Priority, Coordination, Audit) are standing jobs in every department. Work items live inside a department, inherit its goal and checks, and start from its calendar or inbox. Anything the charter does not cover goes to the department head. This is closest to Core today, so it is the cheapest to build, and a founder can read the org chart at a glance. It grows from one step as a department of one. The cost is that a department owns each document, so work that crosses departments needs asks, and a single piece of work cannot exist without a department around it.

The lending company: Collections owns the overdue-loans list and runs a daily reminder routine, and Underwriting owns credit decisions. A write-off above the charter's limit waits for the Collections head to approve it.

B · Flat pipeline

like A spreadsheet of formulas: each cell recalculates when the cells it reads change, and there are no teams.

There is no grouping. The company is one shared ledger of documents and a flat list of pieces of work. Each piece of work carries its own card: its goal, its check, the documents it reads and writes, its clock and wake-ups, its rules and its cap. When a document gets a new version, the work that reads it runs, and the chain brake counts the steps. One company journal records everything. It starts from one step with nothing extra, and every rule sits next to the work it governs. The cost is that at a hundred pieces of work the founder faces a long list with no shape, and the same rule gets copied onto many cards.

The lending company: A new loan application wakes the credit-score work. Its score document wakes the decision work, and the approval wakes the offer-letter work. Each card carries its own cap and rules, and nothing is named Underwriting.

C · Goal projects

like A consulting engagement or a film crew: a team forms around one goal, works from one case file, and disbands when the goal is met.

Work is grouped by goal, not by function. A project opens on request with a brief (goal, checks, rules, caps and a lead) and a case file of documents. Inside the project, each step wakes when its input arrives. When the goal is met, the case file is sealed and archived with its history, and the project dissolves. Nothing sits idle, and every document sits next to the reason it exists. The weak spot is standing work. The daily collections run and the monthly close have no natural home, so each becomes a project that never ends, which is a department under another name. Reading across many closed case files is also hard.

The lending company: Each large loan is a project. Its case file holds the application, the credit memo and the approval, the underwriting lead approves exceptions, and the file is sealed at disbursal. The monthly close is a project reopened every month.

D · Open task board

like A freelance marketplace or a taxi dispatch queue: work is posted, and whoever is free and qualified picks it up.

There is one shared board. Every trigger (a request, a clock tick or a new version) posts a ticket with a goal, an acceptance check and a spend limit. Any person or AI that the company rulebook allows can claim a ticket. They read and write documents in the shared ledger and close the ticket by passing its check. Every ask and every undo goes to one approvals desk. Load balances well, and nothing has to be designed up front. The costs are that no one owns an outcome over time, the single desk becomes a bottleneck as the company grows, and the founder sees a queue rather than a company.

The lending company: The overnight tick posts 400 'chase overdue loan' tickets, and collection AIs and agents claim them. A 'waive late fee' ask lands on the shared desk, where a finance lead approves it.

E · Documents own themselves

like A land registry, or a protected Wikipedia page: the record itself says who may change it, how, and what needs a sign-off.

The documents are the company. Each document carries its own rules: who may add a version, what is refused, what needs approval, how hard an undo is, what a good version must pass, and what any run that writes it may spend. It keeps its own history and a list of the work to wake when it changes. Work is simply whatever adds a version, and it must satisfy the document it writes. This makes documents first-class and safe by default. The costs are that rules scatter across thousands of documents, it is hard to see who does what, and a goal bigger than one document has nowhere to live.

The lending company: The loan book document says only the disbursal work may add rows and that write-offs over a limit need Finance to approve them. Each new version wakes the collections-priority list, and each borrower's file carries its own undo rules.

Leading: E · Documents own themselves (+1 against the datum). The majority scores side with the refuter: documents that own their rules lead, departments are the datum.

The strongest counter: Departments were not derived from the capabilities. They came from Core, and the roadmap was then written in their terms, so the scoring went in a circle. The NEXT list is titled "the first department grows", and "cross-department reads" was pushed to LATER. That is why the judges could say A's main cost "does not show up in the first build". The Pugh totals tied at 0 for all five options, and against A, E came out +1. So A won on cheapness and legibility, not on capabilities, and the cheapness is overstated because the versioned document store has to be built new anyway. Departments are the answer to Q1 (legible) and Q4 (scales), and as a grouping they are right. What is wrong is letting a department own documents. In lending, one credit decision is read on day one by disbursal (Finance) and by risk ranking (Collections), so crossing departments is everyday work, not a LATER case.

The hybrid that answers it: Flip which layer sits underneath. The base is B's single company ledger: every document there is versioned and carries E's own rules (who may add a version, what is refused, what a good version must pass, who signs off, how hard an undo is). Work is a set of B cards with named reads and writes, wake-on-version, a cap per run, and one company journal. One chain id and step count run across the whole company. A's departments sit on top as the grouping the founder reads. A department is a named folder of work cards plus the defaults those cards inherit (goal, caps, schedule) and a default person who approves. It never owns a document. Core carries over: a charter becomes the inherited defaults, a routine becomes a card with a clock, and an ask becomes a sign-off. Lending example: the loan book is one ledger document whose rules say only disbursal work may add rows and a write-off above the limit needs Finance's sign-off. Collections is a folder holding the daily-reminder card and the priority-list card, and both read the loan book. One brake counts the chain as it moves from Collections to Finance and back. Growth follows the ruled model: the start is one document, one card and one output, and a department folder appears only when a second card needs the same defaults. That keeps the org chart and fixes A's problems with crossing teams, lineage, the brake, and documents coming first.

4 · What the fields already know

Gall's law, and why it holds

John Gall, General Systemantics, 1975 (reissued as Systemantics, 1977); Herbert Simon, The Architecture of Complexity, Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society, 1962 · confidence: high on the sources; Gall's is a heuristic, Simon's the argument

Gall's aphorism: a complex system that works is invariably found to have evolved from a simple system that worked. Simon gives the reason: complex systems form far faster when they are built through stable intermediate forms, each of which works on its own.

What it gives Native: every step on this page is a stable intermediate form: the seed works, the chain works, the junction works, before anything else is added.

The narrow waist

National Research Council, Realizing the Information Future, 1994; Micah Beck, On the hourglass model, Communications of the ACM, 2019; Akhshabi and Dovrolis, SIGCOMM, 2011 · confidence: high on the sources; moderate on the mapping

One thin layer in the middle, with many things above and below it, lets both sides change on their own. Beck argues the waist must be minimally sufficient: no more than the things above it need. Akhshabi and Dovrolis show such a waist tends to emerge, and then to freeze.

What it gives Native: the artifact contract is the waist: kept minimal, and changed only as a one-way door.

Where functions belong

Saltzer, Reed and Clark, End-to-end arguments in system design, ACM Transactions on Computer Systems, 1984 · confidence: high

A function that only the ends can do correctly belongs at the ends, not in the shared layer between them.

What it gives Native: the goal, check, gate, cap and trigger are carried by each piece of work, not built into the contract; the contract only moves and records artifacts.

Same kit, different reading

Lewis Wolpert, Positional information and the spatial pattern of cellular differentiation, Journal of Theoretical Biology, 1969 · confidence: high on the source; the growth reading is an analogy

Every cell carries the same genome; what it becomes depends on where it sits.

What it gives Native: every piece carries the same kit from birth, and what it grows depends on the terrain its record shows; Wolpert is about position, not growth over time, so this is an analogy.

Parts at different stages, linked

Simon Wardley, Wardley maps, from about 2005 · confidence: high on the source; note the direction is the opposite of 'grown' here

Every component of a business sits somewhere from genesis to commodity; components at different stages coexist and link. In Wardley's sense the evolved end is the standard, boring one.

What it gives Native: the contract belongs at the commodity end; a part that settles and crystallizes into a tool moves towards it.

Piecemeal growth

Christopher Alexander and others, The Oregon Experiment, 1975 · confidence: moderate

A campus grows well through many small local acts, each repairing what a diagnosis of use shows is needed, under a few shared patterns, rather than through a master plan.

What it gives Native: growth is pulled piece by piece by the record, under the shared contract and rules.

2 · What the fields already know

Several fields built this independently. That is the best sign the idea is sound. One rival shows the road not taken.

Place/transition nets

Carl Adam Petri, dissertation, 1962; workflow nets: van der Aalst, 1997-1998 · confidence: high

A net has two kinds of node: places, which hold tokens, and transitions, which fire. A transition fires only when every place it reads holds a token; firing takes tokens from its inputs and puts tokens in its outputs. Petri drew places as circles and transitions as thin bars on the arrows, which is the flipped picture on this page. Van der Aalst used workflow nets for business processes and proved when one is sound: every case that starts can finish.

What it gives Native: artifacts are places, work is transitions; a junction's rule is the firing rule; soundness is a check the whole landscape can pass or fail before it runs.

Artifact-centric business processes

Nigam and Caswell, IBM Systems Journal, 2003; Bhattacharya, Hull et al., 2007; Hull, survey, 2008 · confidence: high on the source; moderate on the adoption claim

IBM's researchers modelled operations around business artifacts, the things a business actually tracks (an order, a claim, an application), each with its own information and life cycle, and treated the work as services that move artifacts along. They found business people read these models more easily than activity flows.

What it gives Native: the direct field for the flip: artifacts first, work as what moves them; each artifact has a life cycle, which is GROW.

Blackboard systems

Hearsay-II, Erman, Hayes-Roth, Lesser and Reddy, ACM Computing Surveys, 1980; Nii, AI Magazine, 1986 · confidence: high

Independent specialists share one blackboard; each writes a partial answer when it can add something, and a control component decides who goes next. The solution assembles itself on the board.

What it gives Native: several pieces of work contributing to one artifact is a blackboard; the Coordinate work is the blackboard's control component.

Stigmergy

Grasse, on termite nest building, 1959; Heylighen, Cognitive Systems Research, 2016 · confidence: high on Grasse; moderate on the generalisation

Termites coordinate through what they leave behind: each ball of mud invites the next. Heylighen argues Wikipedia and open-source code work the same way, each edit inviting the next.

What it gives Native: work never calls work; a new artifact is the trace that wakes the next work, which is why the landscape can grow without a central plan.

Build systems

Feldman, Make, Software: Practice and Experience, 1979 · confidence: high

Files are the nodes; rules say how to turn some files into another; a file is rebuilt only when what it depends on changed.

What it gives Native: the everyday proof that artifacts-as-nodes, work-as-edges runs at scale; it also gives the rule for when work should wake: when an input artifact has a new version.

The rival: actors

Hewitt, Bishop and Steiger, IJCAI, 1973 · confidence: high

Each actor has a mailbox and sends messages straight to other actors; there is no shared place.

What it gives Native: the design Native does not take: direct messages leave no artifact between two pieces of work, and a company needs that record.

3 · The landscape, one axis at a time

One department, a lending company's collections, drawn nine times. Each picture adds one thing and keeps everything before it. Amber boxes are artifacts, blue chips are work, the dashed green area is the world.

v1 · Bare: artifacts, and the work between them

An artifact (amber box) is information that exists: a loan book, a due list, a plan, a reply. Work (the blue chip on the arrows) turns artifacts into new artifacts; every piece of work starts at artifacts and ends at artifacts. Coordinate reads two artifacts and writes one, which is why work is drawn as a chip where the arrows meet, not as a bare line. The world is where some work reaches out and where replies come back from.

v2 · Add SHAPE: every piece of work carries its goal, its check and its impact

Before a piece of work runs, SHAPE reads its input artifacts and fixes the goal, the check that says it is done, and the impact it must move (ruled 2026-09-25). The artifacts do not change; the work carries the intent.

v3 · Add GOVERN: the gate is a property of the work

Publishing the outreach plan is routine, so Coordinate runs on auto. Calling a borrower leaves the record and cannot be undone, so that work waits for a person's stamp before it runs.

v4 · Add EDGE: the work is the edge, and a junction is an artifact with its coordinator

Two pieces of work, Score risk and Plan outreach, each write their own contribution; Coordinate merges them and publishes the outreach plan only when both are in. The plan and the work around it are one junction you can zoom into. Nothing writes the same artifact twice at once.

v5 · Add RESOURCE: what each piece of work may spend

The work that spends carries a bar: calls today for the call, model tokens for scoring and planning. Work whose bar is full waits; that is how one new artifact is kept from waking a flood of work.

v6 · Add TIME: when each piece of work wakes

Intake runs daily at 09:00; the review runs weekly; a reply is expected within 48 hours. Time decides when work wakes; the junction's rule decides whether it may.

v7 · Add GROW: artifacts keep every version

Work never edits an artifact in place; it writes the next version. The promise ledger and the outreach plan keep their history, so the review reads a history, not a snapshot. That accumulated record is what GROW measures.

v8 · Add the moves: Realization down, Reflection up

Down: any piece of work opens into its own artifacts and smaller work, and work that repeats the same way becomes a tool, work crystallized. Up: the review finds a pattern in the promises and lifts it into a department rule. The rules box is the department's Identity.

v9 · Add the stages: Decide, Delegate, Deliver, Propose

The department's rules narrow into every piece of work (Delegate). An engine runs each piece and its check flips (Deliver). A person stamps before the call leaves for the world (Decide). The review sends its rule up as an ask (Propose). The whole frame, on one landscape of artifacts and work.

4 · Where decisions are made

Artifacts do not decide; they hold what is known. Every decision is work: it reads artifacts (the facts, the options, the rules) and chooses, on the way from one artifact to the next, against the goal that piece of work carries. A decision is not a third kind of thing. The decisions that matter write their choice down as an artifact of their own, so the next piece of work can read it and anyone can later ask why.

What the fields say. Herbert Simon (The New Science of Management Decision, 1960) split a decision into intelligence, design and choice: a decision is a process, which is to say work. OMG's Decision Model and Notation (DMN 1.0, 2015) models decisions apart from the process, each with its input data and its knowledge source: here, the input artifacts and the department rules. In a Petri net, a place with two outgoing transitions is a conflict; only one fires, and a guard on the artifact decides which. Decision records (Nygard, 2011) are the practice of keeping the choice as an artifact. Confidence: high that each exists as described; moderate on the mapping, which is this page's reading.

A decision is work that writes its choice down. If anyone will later ask why, the choice needs an artifact; if no one will, it can stay inside the work.

The chain brake, simply. Wake-on-a-new-version means one change can set off a chain: the loan book changes, Intake writes a new due list, the new due list wakes whatever reads it, and so on. The brake gives the whole chain one id (its root: the loan book version that started it) and counts its steps. A piece of work never runs twice for the same root, so nothing loops, and a chain that grows past its limit stops and asks.
